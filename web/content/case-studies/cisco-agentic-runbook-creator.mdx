## Overview

The Agentic Runbook Creator, or ARC, automates troubleshooting for industrial
network faults by allowing for dynamic creation of runbooks, which are automated scripts made to troubleshoot network faults. During my Cisco internship on the Industrial IoT team, I worked
on the reasoning, generation, privacy, integration, and evaluation paths that
turned a fault context into a testable runbook instead of selecting only from a
fixed procedure catalog.

## Problem

The existing workflow covered 13 predefined procedures. Faults outside that
catalog still required engineers to assemble hypotheses and diagnostic steps
manually (which could take hours depending on the fault at hand),
while sensitive topology details could not simply be forwarded to a
cloud model.

<Callout>
  ARC had to generate situation-specific guidance without ever losing the privacy boundary of the client network. That single requirement is why the pipeline splits into local reasoning and cloud synthesis at all.
</Callout>

## Local reasoning and staged generation

I contributed to local small-language-model reasoning and a multi-stage runbook
generation flow. Local processing established the initial fault context and turned it
into hypotheses as to what the problem could be. Before cloud analysis, the handoff obfuscated sensitive topology
information so later LLM reasoning could use the structure of the problem without
exposing the original network details. The second part of the pipeline was the runbook generation,
which used the hypotheses bundle to produce a runbook that could hypothetically troubleshoot every hypothesis posed.

A Flask integration layer connected those stages and kept the boundary between
local and cloud work explicit. The goal was not an unrestricted model call; it
was a repeatable workflow whose inputs and transitions could be controlled.

## Evaluation loop

The final part of the pipeline, which I built directly, was a digital-twin evaluator that injected faults into Cisco IOS XE network
simulations and exercised the generated runbooks. This made evaluation part of
the system rather than a manual review performed only after generation.

The evaluator provided a path toward closed-loop troubleshooting inside the
client network: generate a candidate runbook, test it against a simulated fault,
and use the observed behavior to decide whether the procedure was useful. It also
integrated an LLM judge that decided whether the runbook was of good enough quality
to send back to the client or if it needed to go through a regeneration phase.

## Results and boundaries

The project expanded troubleshooting beyond the 13 predefined procedures and reduced triage time from hours to under 25 minutes. Those are
project-level results; my individual contribution was the local reasoning,
multi-stage generation, privacy-preserving handoff, Flask integration, and
digital-twin evaluation work described above.

## Takeaways

Interning at Cisco was one of the most eye-opening experiences of my life.
The idea of working this extensively with AI while still claiming ownership of what
it generated was a new concept for me. Furthermore, I worked on this project with two
other interns (Srivishnu Vusirikala and Pranav Butaney), and learning how to work with
others on a project as intricate and influential as this one was amazing. Building the
evaluator itself taught me that I couldn't accept just a syntactically sound runbook -
the semantics of the runbook itself mattered way more. Furthermore, the privacy layer
was a change from how I usually work. Now, instead of just making sure that the project worked
with the data given, I had to make sure that the data itself was properly cleansed and that
the evaluator could work with said obfuscated identifiers. Other than that, I had
all the essentials for the best internship experience, and I'd love coming back!
